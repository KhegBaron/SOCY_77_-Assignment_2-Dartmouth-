01   The Model

We trust numbers
because they feel precise.

Classical finance offers a clean answer to the question of value. A company is worth the present value of its future cash flows all future earnings discounted back to today, adjusted for risk and the passage of time.

This is the Discounted Cash Flow model, the dominant valuation framework taught in every business school on earth. It processes uncertainty and returns a single, precise number.

Value = Σ [ CFt ÷ (1 + r)t ]
where CF = future cash flows    r = discount rate    t = time period

The formula signals rigor. It implies that someone ran the math. And so $10,000,000 stops being an estimate and starts being a fact.

03   The Rupture

Early-stage startups have
profits. revenues. track records.

The DCF model requires inputs that startups do not have. No cash flows to discount. No history to project from. The formula still runs, but it runs on fiction.

"Startup valuation is closer to alchemy than to science." Miloud, Aspelund and Cabrol (2012)

When the model fails, something else steps in to produce the number. That something is not objective. It is deeply social.

04   The Social Machine

What actually
sets the number?

When fundamentals are absent, valuation becomes a social negotiation — shaped by reputation, narrative, and peer comparison rather than cash flow arithmetic.

Research by Imbierowicz and Rauch (2024) finds that startup valuations are primarily driven by peer firm comparisons, not fundamental analysis. The number anchors to what similar companies raised, a socially constructed baseline, not an independently derived truth.

Investor reputation and VC affiliation further inflate or deflate valuations independent of any company-level data. The prestige of the investor becomes part of the asset's price.

Error rates extend from 68% to over 113% depending on the model used. Even the most sophisticated tools produce answers that are, in the best case, roughly half-wrong.

The number $10,000,000 carries false authority. Its precision, six zeroes, no decimal, implies a certainty the underlying method cannot support.

07   The Reframe

A different number
tells a different truth.

We began with $10,000,000 — clean, authoritative, final. We now understand that number not as a measurement, but as a social performance.

A startup "valued at $10 million" is more accurately described as a startup whose value is plausibly anywhere between $5 million and $20 million, depending on whose assumptions, whose comparables, and whose network generated the estimate.

This reframing does not change the number. It changes what the number is allowed to claim. It exposes the gap between precision (what the number performs) and accuracy (what the number can support).

Valuation is not discovered. It is constructed from peer comparisons, investor narratives, reputational signals, and social negotiations that happen before a single formula is run. The $10M figure is the output of this process, laundered through arithmetic into apparent objectivity.

Safe Superintelligence was co-founded by Ilya Sutskever, one of the most recognized names in AI research and a former OpenAI chief scientist. Investors include Sequoia Capital, Andreessen Horowitz, and SV Angel. The company has no product, no revenue, and no commercial roadmap for the near term. Its roughly 20 researchers are focused entirely on fundamental AI safety work.

A $32 billion valuation for a company with no product is not a measurement of present value.
It is a bet on a narrative.

The SSI case makes the theoretical argument concrete. What drove the sixfold increase from $5B to $32B in seven months? Not revenue. Not profits. Not a product launch. What drove it was founder reputation (Sutskever), investor prestige (Sequoia, a16z), peer comparisons (OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI) and an ambient cultural narrative about the existential importance of safe AI. Every social driver identified by Imbierowicz and Rauch (2024) appears here, and none of them require a DCF model to run.

The $32B number is not wrong. But it is not a discovery. It is a social fact, constructed by networks, reputation, and narrative, then dressed in the language of finance to appear inevitable.

The number did not change.

Your understanding of it did.

Startup valuations are not objective measures of worth. They are uncertain, socially produced estimates shaped by assumption, comparison, and the performative authority of numbers themselves. The six zeroes are not precision. They are persuasion.
